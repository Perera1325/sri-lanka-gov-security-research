# Abstract and index terms

## Abstract

Sri Lankan government institutions have experienced a series of publicly reported website
and infrastructure security incidents in recent years, yet little is published about how
these events are followed up or about the state of the institutions' public web security.
This paper presents an exploratory, public-evidence-based study of ten Sri Lankan
government digital institutions. Using only publicly available information and ordinary,
passive browser access, it (i) compiles and classifies a dataset of 20 historical incident
records, distinguishing website compromises from backend incidents, impersonation,
shared-infrastructure failures, and unverified reports; (ii) maps the 32 requirements of
the Sri Lanka CERT web-application security guideline against how far each can be verified
by an outside observer; and (iii) reviews the academic literature on the security of
AI-generated code and maps its reported weakness classes to the OWASP Top 10, without
claiming that any studied website uses such code. Across the ten institutions, six website
compromises affecting five institutions are confirmed from public reporting; for none of
the thirteen confirmed incidents was a published investigation outcome found. Only seven of
the 32 guideline requirements can be informed at all from public evidence, and twenty-one
require written authorization or internal access to check. No active testing was performed,
no current exploitable weakness is claimed, and the project's evidence register is empty by
design. The study's contribution is a transparent, reproducible method and a baseline
dataset for public accountability research, together with an explicit account of what such
research can and cannot establish.

## Index Terms

Government cybersecurity, public-sector web security, Sri Lanka, security governance,
